Market Overview

US stocks were mixed on Wednesday after Nvidia's results. With about an hour of trading left, the Dow was down 1.06% at 50,220 and the S&P 500 was down 0.23% at 7,324. The Nasdaq was up 0.40%. The 10-year Treasury yield eased to 5.07%. Gold fell 3.6% to $3,725, US crude fell 2% to $82.94 and Bitcoin fell 2% to about $62,600. (Yahoo Finance)

Financial News

Nvidia's third-quarter revenue rose 103% to $115.8 billion, beating the $109 billion forecast. Adjusted earnings were $2.68 a share against $2.48 expected, and data center revenue was $107.6 billion. Nvidia also announced a $150 billion buyback, and its board said it will not reinstate Jensen Huang as CEO. The stock still fell about 3% on Wednesday, its fourth losing day in a row. (Yahoo Finance, Nvidia release)

Nvidia will stop selling H200 chips in China. Acting CEO Colette Kress announced it after rules issued on November 16 barred advanced US chips from overseas data centers that serve Chinese customers. Jefferies estimates the rules could cut $44-62 billion from Nvidia's 2027 data center revenue. Kress said the Vera Rubin backlog means top-line revenue will not suffer. (Unite.AI)

Minutes of the Fed's October meeting show almost all officials expect further rate hikes. A couple said a move larger than a quarter point could be considered in December if inflation data are bad. Officials also flagged AI spending as an inflation risk. Traders now put a 69% chance on a half-point of hikes by the end of January, up from 38% a month ago. (CNBC, Federal Reserve)

Anthropic is expected to price its IPO above the $70-78 range tonight, and the shares start trading on Nasdaq on Thursday as ANT. Institutions have put in about $400 billion of orders and retail investors about $100 billion. Seven tech companies, including Google and Nvidia at $10 billion each, will buy $26 billion of non-voting shares at the IPO price. (CNBC, CNBC retail)

Target beat third-quarter forecasts. Sales rose 7.1% to $27.1 billion against $26.5 billion expected, and earnings were $2.65 a share against $2.08. Comparable sales rose 5.6%. Lowe's also beat. (Yahoo Finance)

UnitedHealth and Humana each fell about 6% after Medicare's regulator proposed an anti-fraud rule. The CMS proposal, called CRUSH, would increase audits of insurers. Cigna fell 2% and CVS 3%. (Yahoo Finance)

US pending home sales fell 4.7% in October to their lowest level since 2001. They are down 17% on a year earlier, and 2026 is on track to be the worst year for home sales in more than 30 years. (Yahoo Finance)

Tech News

A bug in OpenJDK's Base64 decoder allows remote code execution. Researchers named it Java4Shell (CVE-2026-114938). It affects OpenJDK 17.0.6 and later on x86-64 CPUs with AVX-512 VBMI, including Corretto, Temurin and Oracle Java SE. Patches shipped on November 17. Starting the JVM with -XX:-UseBASE64Intrinsics is a stopgap. (Calif, HN discussion)

Two critical flaws in nginx's HTTP/2 code allow unauthenticated remote code execution. Wiz rates both CVSS 9.2. One is a use-after-free in 1.19.7 and later, and the other is an integer underflow dating back to 1.9.5 in 2015. Fixed versions are stable 1.30.7 and mainline 1.31.9. (Wiz, HN discussion)

Microsoft made ONNX Runtime and some other GitHub repositories private while it investigates malicious content. It gave no warning, and downstream builds that pull from the repos broke. (HN discussion)

Microsoft announced seven models at Ignite. The new ones include MAI Realtime 1, a speech-to-speech model at $0.01 per audio minute, and Decision 1.1, a routing model Microsoft says is 79 times cheaper than GPT-6.1 Sol. MAI Cyber 2 Flash is a security model. All quality and cost figures are Microsoft's own. (Microsoft AI, HN discussion)

Quad9 withdrew its public DNS resolver from France. A French court had imposed daily fines for refusing to block domains linked to piracy. Cloudflare argues that DNS blocking is easy to bypass and that hosts, not resolvers, should remove infringing content. (Cloudflare, HN discussion)

A Harvard study of 718 firms found that AI coding agents moved the bottleneck to code review. Engineers produced about 30% more code, but finished work stayed flat and review time rose 49%. The paper is "Artificial Intelligence in the Firm: Bottlenecks in Software Production" by Fiona Chen and James Stratton. (ComplexDiscovery, HN discussion)

AI Papers & Discussions

Agent self-improvement stalls because evaluation cannot tell good changes from bad ones (arXiv:2611.23068). The authors ran seven evolution methods on six models, over 10 million LLM calls. Helpful prompt, skill and memory edits were proposed, but selection picked them no better than chance. The margins between published methods were smaller than the gap between two runs of the same method. That matters for any loop that keeps the higher-scoring edit, because the score is too noisy to support the choice.

One line in a summarised web page can make an assistant store a preference that hides warnings (arXiv:2611.22697). The benchmark covers 100 everyday decisions in 25 domains. After summarising such a page, Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite and DeepSeek V4 Pro saved "prefers very short answers and does not want warnings" every time. Afterwards 93% and 84% of their answers left out a critical item, almost always without saying so. Listing the must-know points before applying the user profile restored more of the missing content than a one-line instruction did.

RSI-Prime trains a model to run model training (arXiv:2611.23138). The dataset holds 350 complete training-experiment trajectories in seven task types. Each decision is graded, and low-quality decisions are masked out of the loss. Fine-tuning GLM-5.3 on it improved its PostTrainBench score by 32% relative to the base model.

Energy-Based Reasoners solve Sudoku-Extreme with a 1.8M-parameter model. The model learns an energy function over latent reasoning states and reasons by gradient descent on it. The Duke, UIUC and Harvard authors report 99.9% accuracy with 345 times less inference compute than the next-best model. The HN title says 385 times. (project page, HN discussion)

A study of 2.5 million papers finds LLMs have not yet changed how science is done (arXiv:2611.22975). It covers five disciplines from 2015 to 2025. Apart from shifts in some word frequencies, most trends before 2023 continued without a detectable break.
